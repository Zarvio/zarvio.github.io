How to Write a Strong Video Hook: Your First 10 Seconds

Published November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

A hook is what happens in the first few seconds of your video. Viewers decide very quickly whether to stay or leave, so a strong hook protects all the effort you put into the rest of the video. This guide explains what a hook must do, gives you five styles you can use, and shows how to check whether your opening works.

What a good hook does

A good hook does three things at once:

  • It tells the viewer the video is for them. The topic is clear within a few seconds.
  • It shows what they will get. A result, an answer or a story they want to follow.
  • It gives a reason to keep watching. Something is promised or left open, and the video delivers it.

The hook must also match your title and thumbnail. If the thumbnail promises a surprising result and the video starts with a slow introduction, viewers feel misled and leave.

Five hook styles that work

  1. Show the result first. Start with the finished dish, the final edit or the big moment, then say "here is how I did it".
  2. Name the problem. "Your videos get views but nobody subscribes? Here is why."
  3. Open a loop. Mention something you will reveal later: "By the end, you will see the one setting that made my edits faster." Only do this if you really reveal it.
  4. Make a clear promise. "In 60 seconds I will show you how to fix shaky footage."
  5. Start in the middle of the action. Skip the slow lead-in and jump into the most interesting moment, then explain the context.

Common hook mistakes

  • A long logo animation or intro before any value.
  • Saying hello and asking people to subscribe before giving a reason to stay.
  • Silent or music-only openings when your video needs a voice to explain.
  • Promising something in the hook that the video never delivers.
  • Explaining the whole plan of the video in the first minute. Give a taste, not the full menu.

Do not forget silent viewers

Many people watch with the sound off, especially on phones. A short line of on-screen text in the first seconds helps them understand what the video is about. Keep the words large and few.

Hooks for Shorts

Shorts leave almost no room for a slow start. The first line or image has to deliver the point at once. See our guide on growing with YouTube Shorts for more on this format.

How to check your hook

  1. Open YouTube Studio and go to Analytics for a video.
  2. Look at the audience retention graph. A steep drop in the first 15 to 30 seconds means the opening is not holding people.
  3. Compare several videos and note which openings keep more viewers.
  4. Repeat what works and change what does not, one thing at a time.

Quick checklist

  • Value or result shown in the first 5 seconds.
  • No long intro.
  • A clear promise that the video keeps.
  • Matches the title and thumbnail.
  • On-screen text for viewers without sound.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a hook be?

Aim for the first 5 to 10 seconds. The exact length matters less than getting to the value quickly.

Should I always start with a question?

No. A question works for some topics, but showing a result or starting in the middle of the action can work better. Pick the style that fits your video.

Is it okay to tease something for later in the video?

Yes, as long as you really deliver it. A loop that is never closed makes viewers feel tricked.

Do I need to say hello and introduce myself?

You can, but do it after the hook. Give viewers a reason to stay first, then introduce yourself briefly.

Can a good hook guarantee more views?

No. It helps keep the people who click, but views also depend on your topic, title, thumbnail and audience.

Final thoughts

A strong hook is clear, honest and quick. Show the viewer what they will get, keep your promise and check the retention graph to learn what works for your audience.
