How to Write AI Video Prompts That Give Better Results

Published November 18, 2026 · 8 min read

AI video tools can turn a few lines of text into a short clip, but the quality of the clip depends heavily on the quality of the prompt. A vague prompt gives a random result. A clear, structured prompt gives you something much closer to what you imagined. This guide shows a practical way to write prompts and plan a whole video.

Why prompts fail

Most weak prompts have the same problems. They are too short ("a cat in a kitchen"), they pack too many actions into one clip, or they leave out the camera and lighting, so the tool has to guess. AI video clips are also short, often around eight seconds, so a prompt that describes a whole story will either be ignored or squashed into a confusing result.

The seven parts of a strong prompt

You do not need to use every part every time, but thinking through them helps.

  1. Subject: who or what is in the shot. Be specific about age, clothing, color and size.
  2. Action: one clear action, in present tense, such as "pours milk into a bowl".
  3. Setting: where and when. A small wooden kitchen at sunrise says much more than "a kitchen".
  4. Camera: shot type and movement, such as close-up, wide shot, slow push in, handheld or still.
  5. Lighting and mood: warm morning light, neon night, soft overcast, tense, calm, playful.
  6. Style: realistic, animated, cinematic, documentary, or a specific look such as watercolor.
  7. Audio or dialogue (if the tool supports it): a short line of speech, ambient sound or music mood.

Weak and strong examples

Weak: A girl cooking in a kitchen.

Strong: A young woman in a yellow apron stirs a pot of soup in a small sunlit kitchen. Steam rises as she tastes the soup and smiles. Medium shot, slow push in, warm morning light, realistic style, soft kitchen ambience.

The strong prompt is not long, but every phrase tells the tool something useful. It has one subject, one action, a clear setting, a camera move and a mood.

Plan the video scene by scene

If you want a video longer than one clip, split it into scenes before you write any prompts. A simple structure for a 30-second video is:

  • Scene 1 (hook): the most interesting moment or question.
  • Scene 2: setup or problem.
  • Scene 3: the turn or main action.
  • Scene 4: the result or payoff.

Write one prompt per scene. Each prompt should stand alone, because the tool does not remember the previous clip unless you give it a reference. Writing a short script first, with one sentence per scene, makes the prompts much easier to write.

Keep characters consistent

The hardest part of multi-scene AI video is making the same character look the same each time. These habits help:

  • Create a character reference image first. Make one image that shows the character clearly, and use it as the starting point or reference in each scene if your tool allows it.
  • Repeat the exact description. Use the same words for clothes, hair and colors in every prompt. Do not say "red jacket" in one and "crimson coat" in the next.
  • Use a first-frame image for each scene when the tool supports image-to-video. It anchors the look much more than text alone.
  • Keep the style line identical across scenes so the lighting and look match.

Use negative prompts carefully

Some tools let you list things to avoid, such as "no text, no watermark, no extra fingers, no blurry faces". These can help, but a long list can also confuse the result. Add only the problems that actually appear in your clips.

Iterate in small steps

When a result is close but not right, change one thing at a time: the camera move, the lighting or the action. If you change five things at once you will not know which one helped. Keep a note of prompts that worked so you can reuse their structure.

Common mistakes

  • Putting several actions in one clip.
  • Using abstract words like "beautiful" or "amazing" without describing what that looks like.
  • Changing character descriptions between scenes.
  • Forgetting the aspect ratio. Decide on 9:16 for Shorts and Reels, or 16:9 for standard YouTube, before you start.
  • Asking for real people, brands or copyrighted characters. Most tools restrict this, and it can create legal problems.
