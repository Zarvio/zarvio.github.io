How to Write a Strong Video Hook: Your First 10 Seconds

Updated November 21, 2026 · 8 min read

A hook is whatever happens in the first few seconds of your video. In that short time, a viewer silently decides whether to stay or leave. A strong hook protects all the work you put into the rest of the video. This guide gives you a simple three-step structure, five hook styles with examples, and a checklist you can run before you publish.

The short version: in the first 10 seconds, (1) show the viewer this video is for them, (2) prove or promise what they will get, and (3) give them a reason to stay. Skip the logo, the long greeting and the "please subscribe" until you have delivered some value.

What the viewer is really asking

When a video starts, the viewer is asking three quick questions, even if they do not say them aloud:

  1. "Is this what I was looking for?" The title and thumbnail brought them here, and the opening must confirm it.
  2. "Is it worth my time?" They want to see a result, a promise or a reason to believe.
  3. "Why should I stay until the end?" They need something that makes leaving feel like a loss.

A good hook answers all three in about ten seconds. Most weak openings answer none of them.

The 3-step hook structure

SecondsJobWhat to do
0 to 3Confirm the topicSay what the video is about, or show the final result, right away.
3 to 7Prove or promiseTell them exactly what they will get, or show a quick proof.
7 to 10Give a reason to stayOpen a loop ("later I will show you...") or start step one immediately.

A simple fill-in template: "[Result or problem] for [who it is for], and in this video I will show you [what they get]."

Five hook styles you can use

  1. Show the result first. Start with the finished dish, the final edit or the big moment, then say "here is how it was made".
  2. Name the problem. "Your videos get views but nobody subscribes? Here is the most common reason."
  3. Open a loop. Mention something you will reveal later: "By the end, you will see the one setting that fixes this."
  4. Make a clear promise. "In 60 seconds I will show you how to fix shaky footage."
  5. Start in the middle of the action. Skip the slow lead-in and jump to the most interesting moment, then go back and explain.

Use one style per video. Trying to use all five in ten seconds will confuse the viewer.

Notice the pattern: the better openings name the topic, show what the viewer gets and avoid greetings. You can still say hello, just after the hook.

Hooks for Shorts

On Shorts, viewers scroll fast, so the opening has even less time. Start with the most interesting frame or sentence immediately, add a few words of on-screen text, and keep the first line short. Because a Short is brief, a loop that makes people want to see the beginning again can also help. Our Shorts growth guide covers length and structure in more detail.

Do not forget silent viewers

Many people watch with the sound off, especially on phones. A short line of on-screen text in the first seconds tells them what the video is about even before they turn the sound on. Use clear, large text and keep it to a few words.

Common hook mistakes

  • A long logo animation or intro before any value is shown.
  • Saying hello and asking people to subscribe before giving a reason to stay.
  • Reading the title out loud word for word, which tells the viewer nothing new.
  • A silent or music-only opening when the video needs a voice to explain.
  • Promising something in the hook that the video never delivers. This is the fastest way to lose trust.
  • Too much setup: three sentences of background before the first useful idea.

Your hook must match your title and thumbnail

If the title promises "5 free editing apps", the first seconds should confirm that, not talk about something else. A mismatch makes viewers feel misled and leave. Write the title, thumbnail and hook as one package. Our title guide and thumbnail guide show how to keep all three consistent.

A 2-minute hook checklist

Give your opening one point for every "yes":

  1. Does the viewer know the topic within 3 seconds?
  2. Is a result, proof or clear promise shown in the first 7 seconds?
  3. Is there a reason to keep watching (a loop or an immediate first step)?
  4. Is it free of a long intro, greeting and subscribe request?
  5. Does it match the title and thumbnail?
  6. Does the video really keep the promise?

6 points: a strong hook. 4 or 5: fix the missing items. 3 or fewer: rewrite the opening using the three-step structure.

How to check if your hook works

  1. Open YouTube Studio, go to Analytics and open the audience retention graph of a video.
  2. Look at the first 15 to 30 seconds. A steep drop right at the start means the opening is not holding people. If your Studio shows a "key moments" section, check the intro figure there too.
  3. Compare several of your videos. Note which openings keep more viewers.
  4. Repeat what works and change one thing at a time, such as removing the intro or showing the result first.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a hook be?

Aim for roughly the first 5 to 10 seconds. The goal is not a fixed number but a clear answer to "is this for me and is it worth my time?" as fast as possible.

Should I put my intro animation before the hook?

Usually no. Start with the hook and add a very short branded moment after it, or skip the intro entirely.

Can I ask viewers to subscribe in the hook?

It is better to wait until you have given some value. Viewers subscribe more willingly after they have seen that the video is useful.

Do I need a hook in every video?

Yes. Even a calm, long video needs a clear opening that tells viewers what they will get.

Can a great hook guarantee more watch time?

No. A hook helps people stay at the start, but they stay to the end only if the rest of the video delivers on the promise.

Final thoughts

A strong hook is clear, quick and honest: it names the topic, shows what the viewer will get and gives a reason to stay. Write it last if that helps, test it with the checklist, and make sure the rest of the video keeps the promise.
