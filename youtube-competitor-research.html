How to Do Competitor Research on YouTube Without Copying

Published November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

Studying other channels in your niche is one of the fastest ways to learn what viewers want. The goal is not to copy anyone. The goal is to understand patterns, find gaps and fill them with your own voice and experience. This guide gives you a simple process you can finish in about an hour, and a short routine to keep it going every week.

Why competitor research is worth your time

Most new creators pick topics by guessing. Competitor research replaces guessing with evidence. If five channels in your niche each have a video on the same question that did far better than their other uploads, that question is something viewers actively want answered. You still have to make a better, clearer or more personal answer, but you start with a topic that already has demand.

It also protects you from wasting weeks on a format that your niche does not respond to, and it shows you what the competition has not covered yet.

Step 1: Pick the right channels

Choose three to five channels that make videos for the same viewer as you. A good mix looks like this:

  • One or two channels about your size. Their strategy is closer to what you can realistically do right now.
  • One or two bigger channels. They show where the niche can go, but remember they have budgets, teams and audiences you may not have yet.
  • One channel that is growing fast. A new channel that is doing well often found a gap everyone else missed.

Do not pick channels only because they are famous. Pick them because their viewers look like your viewers.

Step 2: Look at their most popular videos

Open the channel's Videos tab and sort by "Popular". Write down the topic of each of the top ten videos. If similar topics appear again and again, viewers clearly want them. If one video is far ahead of the rest, ask why: was it the topic, the title, a trend at that time, or a big external share?

Also look at the newest videos. Popular videos show what worked in the past, and recent uploads show what the channel believes works now.

Step 3: Study titles and thumbnails

Look at how the top videos are named and what the thumbnails show. You are looking for patterns, not sentences to copy. Ask yourself:

  • Do the titles use numbers, questions, a specific result or a time limit?
  • How long are the titles, and what comes first?
  • Does the thumbnail show a face, an object, a before and after, or only a few words?

Use the idea behind a pattern, never the exact wording. Our guide on writing a YouTube title that gets clicks honestly explains how to turn a pattern into a title that is truly yours and still accurate.

Step 3b: Listen to the first seconds

Watch the first ten seconds of three popular videos. Notice how quickly they tell the viewer what the video is about and what the viewer will get. This is the hook, and it often decides whether people stay. Read our guide to writing a strong hook and compare it with what you see.

Step 4: Compare tags and descriptions

The public tags and description of a video show how its creator describes it. Comparing a competitor's tags with yours can reveal related phrases you did not think of. Keep one rule: add only tags that honestly match your video. Tags play a small role on YouTube, and misleading tags can break the platform's rules. Our YouTube tags guide explains how to use them sensibly.

Step 5: Check upload habits

How often does the channel post, and on which days? A steady schedule is often a quiet reason behind steady growth. Do not copy their exact days without thinking. Your own audience may be in a different time zone or may watch at different hours, so also read our guide on the best time to upload.

Step 6: Find the gaps

This is where the real value is. Read the comments on the popular videos and look for:

  • Questions that nobody answered.
  • Requests such as "can you explain this more simply?"
  • Complaints about quality, such as bad audio, outdated information or rushed explanations.
  • Topics that people ask for in the comments but that the channel never made.

Each of these is an idea for your next video, and you already know there is an audience for it.

A simple example

The table below is an illustrative example only, not real channel data. It shows how you might record what you notice for a made-up niche, "budget home cooking".

What I checkedWhat I noticedMy idea (in my own words)
Top 10 videosMost are about cooking with very few ingredientsA video on meals with five ingredients, made with my own recipes
TitlesMany include a number and a clear resultTest titles that state a number and the real outcome

Keep it ethical

  • Never reuse someone's video, script, thumbnail or music.
  • Use research for ideas, then add your own experience, examples and style.
  • Credit your sources when you use someone else's information.
  • Do not copy a channel's name, branding or exact title formula.

Copying also does not work well for growth. Viewers who already watched the original have little reason to watch a copy.

A simple weekly routine

  1. Spend thirty minutes once a week checking the newest uploads of your chosen channels.
  2. Write down one topic, one title pattern and one audience question.
  3. Pick one idea to test in your next video.
  4. After a few weeks, check your own numbers. In YouTube Studio, see where viewers drop off using our guide on reading audience retention.

Over a few months this habit gives you a clear view of your niche and a list of tested ideas.

Frequently asked questions

How many competitors should I study?

Three to five is enough. More than that usually gives you too many notes and no clear decision.

Is it okay to use the same topic as a competitor?

Yes. Topics are not owned by anyone. What you must not copy is their video, script, thumbnail or exact wording. Give the topic your own examples, structure and opinion.

Should I copy their tags?

Do not copy blindly. Use their tags as a source of related phrases, then keep only the ones that honestly describe your video.

How often should I do this research?

A full review once a month and a short thirty-minute check every week is plenty for most creators.

Can competitor research guarantee more views?

No. It helps you choose better topics and spot gaps, but views also depend on your content, your audience and how viewers react to your video.

Final thoughts

Good competitor research is closer to listening than to copying. You learn what viewers ask for, then answer it in a way only you can. Start small: pick three channels, write down what you notice and make one video from the best gap you find.
